# Research: defending a room against an entry (the doorway ambush)

Primary-source research for the defenders' leader ordering an ambush of the doorway the attack will come through. It
covers a crew of 2–5 defenders holding a house against an entry team of 3–5. Scale used for the game column: 1 px ≈
1 cm. Doctrine writes defence at platoon scale (a building, a block). Reading it down to a fire team in one house is
an inference, marked as such where it matters.

## 1. Concentrate on the breach, or keep all-round watch

- **All-round security is the first priority, not an option.** "The first priority is establishing all-around
  security. Each position should have at least one soldier providing security during all preparations." Positions
  "should be mutually supporting and have fields of fire in all directions" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30b(1), (5), p. 5-44).
  "A position without mutual support in one building is vulnerable to bypass, isolation, and subsequent destruction
  from any direction" (¶5-30b(3)).
- **Why: the enemy gets in behind.** "Enemy infiltration or movement sometimes occurs between and behind friendly
  positions. Therefore, each position must be organized for all-round defense." (FM 90-10-1 ¶4-14d, p. 4-14.) Secure
  the other ways in: "sewers, basements, stairwells, and rooftops" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30a(4)).
- **But the free men do go to the fight.** The platoon in a strongpoint "organizes all-round defense, and
  counterattacks … to eject an enemy that has a foothold … **Unengaged elements should be ready to counterattack,
  fight fires, or reinforce other elements of the platoon.**" (FM 90-10-1 ¶4-26, 4-26a, p. 4-28; the same words in
  MCWP 3-35.3 ¶3401a, p. 3-36.) Interior routes exist "to engage enemy forces from any direction" (FM 3-06.11
  ¶5-30d(3), p. 5-47).
- **One avenue, one owner.** "Responsibility for primary avenues of approach should never be split" (FM 90-10-1
  ¶4-14b). So the doorway being attacked belongs to one group under one leader.
- **Even an ambush keeps its back covered.** "Establish rear security behind the assault element"; a security element
  "isolates the ambush site" (FM 3-21.8 ¶7-124, ¶7-125, p. 7-27).
- **Feints and second entries are expected.** Police courses teach the **split stack** and **opposing doors** (BART
  §II.6–7). Marines are told to "avoid windows and doors" and "make new entrances" (MCWP 3-35.3 App. A §IV, p. A-17).
  Defence planners ask "Where in the defense do we want the enemy to think he is successful?" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-33c(1)).
- **Compartments eat men.** At Stalingrad "more combatants were required to fill or watch those compartments"
  (Lewis, p. 42). This is the cost the all-round rule pays in a house.

**Reading for a 2–5 crew (inference):** concentrating on the threatened doorway is doctrinal for the *unengaged*
members. Leaving no one on the other approaches is not. Doctrine keeps at least one watcher or a rear guard.

## 2. Where to post to cover a doorway

- **Defenders watch the door; attackers know it.** "The door is the focal point of anyone in the room. It is known as
  the fatal funnel, because it focuses attention at the precise point where the individual team members are the most
  vulnerable." (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-21a, p. 3-27.) "Doorways should not be used as entrances or exits since they are
  normally covered by enemy fire" (FM 90-10-1 ¶5-4, p. 5-3).
- **Off the line of the opening.** For the team holding a room, "Points of domination should not be in front of doors
  or windows so team members are not silhouetted to the outside of the room" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-22c(9), p. 3-32). A
  defender's post obeys the same geometry.
- **Back from the opening, low.** At a window "the soldier is well back into the room to prevent the muzzle flash from
  being seen, and he is kneeling to limit exposure". At a loophole "he stays well back … so the muzzle of the weapon
  does not protrude beyond the wall" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-28c, d, pp. 3-48–3-49). Firing standing "exposes himself at the
  height the enemy would expect a target to appear" (¶3-28a(2)). **No source gives a distance from a doorway.**
  "Well back" is the only measure.
- **Not guessable from outside.** "Screen or block windows and other openings … so that the enemy cannot tell which
  openings the defenders are behind" (FM 90-10-1 ¶4-14d(2)). "Avoid setting a pattern" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-29e). Interior
  loopholes "should enable overwatch of stairs, halls, and unoccupied rooms and be concealed by pictures, drapes, or
  furniture" (MCWP 3-35.3 App. D, p. D-6). That is, cover the approach from the next room or through a wall, not
  only from inside the room being entered.
- **Cross-fire.** "Support each individual fighting position with several others" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-27a(2), p. 5-40).
  Weapons are paired "so that the same target can be engaged from different positions" (¶5-30e).
- **What the entry checks first (indirect evidence).** The first man goes "deep into the near corner" (FM 3-06.11
  ¶3-21b). The police drill has the man on the hinge side "clear behind door" (JLE 140S lab I.A–B). **Inference:** a
  post tight in a corner on the door's own wall is the first spot an entry runs into. It gets the first shot at the
  man crossing, at contact range. A post deeper along the far walls sees the threshold earlier but is easier to see
  while the attackers slice the pie.

## 3. Early warning: acting on sound before contact

- **Detection uses every sense.** "OPs, patrols, and individual soldiers look and listen using night vision devices,
  binoculars, and early warning systems to detect the enemy's approach." On detection, the platoon leader "alerts the
  platoon sergeant, squad leaders … reports the situation … if possible, calls in OP's" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-31b–c, p. 5-49).
  The leader's first task in occupying a building is an OP of "at least two personnel" (¶5-30c, p. 5-45).
- **Hearing is named.** "All of the senses should be used to acquire targets, especially hearing … Marines soon
  recognize the sounds of vehicles and people moving through streets that are littered with rubble." People moving "in
  the halls of a damaged building create more noise" (MCWP 3-35.3 App. A ¶28b, f, pp. A-52–A-53). But "locating the source
  of sounds becomes more difficult because of the natural echoing in built-up areas" (App. H ¶5e). Once the fight
  starts, "the noise of built-up area combat … mak[es] sound signals difficult to hear" (FM 90-10-1 ¶4-14e).
- **The attacker's drill assumes he can be heard.** "Move tactically and silently … Arrive undetected at the entry"
  (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-18, p. 3-24). Kicking a door "rarely works the first time, thereby giving any enemy units within the
  room ample warning (and giving the enemy time to shoot through the door)" (MCWP 3-35.3 App. A ¶19b, p. A-28).
- **The ambush pattern: report, alert, wait, initiate.** "When the target approaches, the security team that spots it
  alerts the ambush leader … the leader alerts the other elements." Then, "when most of the enemy force is in the kill
  zone, the leader initiates the ambush" (FM 3-21.8 ¶9-94–9-95, pp. 9-16–9-17). The leader "must be prepared to give
  free passage to enemy forces that are too large" (¶9-115).

## 4. The leader's place and how orders are given

- **Small-unit leaders fight.** The team leader "is a fighting leader who leads his team by example" and "controls …
  its rate and distribution of fire" (FM 3-21.8 ¶1-78, p. 1-16). In an ambush "the platoon leader is normally located
  with the assault element" and initiates it with the most casualty-producing weapon (¶7-122; ¶9-116). Higher up, "the
  squad leader will position himself to best control his squad" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30c).
- **Planned centrally, fought by the small units.** "Planning for the defense of an urban area must be detailed and
  centralized. As in the offense, execution is decentralized as the battle develops, and the enemy forces assault the
  buildings and rooms." (FM 3-06.11 §II intro, p. 5-3.) "Squad and platoon leaders are often responsible for fighting
  independent battles" (¶5-12c(10), p. 5-15).
- **Sectors are set in advance.** "Leaders will designate for each weapons system a primary and an alternate sector of
  fire" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30d(9)). Fire "shifts to the secondary sector on order, when there are no targets in the
  primary sector" (FM 3-21.8 ¶2-71, p. 2-15). The defence's answer to a threat from a new direction is a **supplementary
  position**: it "covers an avenue of approach or sector of fire different from those covered by the primary position"
  and is "occupied based on specific enemy actions" (FM 3-21.8 ¶8-20, p. 8-4). It is prepared and rehearsed beforehand
  (¶8-46). So the on-the-spot order is doctrinal in its trigger. Doctrine would have planned it before the fight.

## 5. Re-positioning when the threat moves

- **Moving in contact is the dangerous part.** "Displacement in contact poses great problems. The platoon leader must
  therefore plan for it thoroughly before the operation and rehearse moving to alternate and supplementary positions
  … he must carefully evaluate the situation whenever displacement in contact becomes necessary" (FM 3-21.8 ¶8-46,
  p. 8-11). The criteria for moving "are tied to an enemy action" (¶8-47).
- **Individual rules.** "Carefully select a new fighting position before leaving an old one … Keep exposure time to a
  minimum" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-29e, p. 3-53). Crossing a doorway: "move quickly through it … staying as low as possible"
  (FM 90-10-1 ¶5-4). Routes between rooms are mouseholed, marked and rehearsed (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30d(3)).
- **Shifting to deceive.** Positions are shifted "just before dark to deceive the enemy as to the exact location of the
  primary position" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-12c(13)).
- **Inference:** doctrine moves defenders on a *confirmed* change of the threat's direction, not on every report. It
  wants the move done before the enemy is at the door. Once the stack is close, a move is both noisy and exposed.

## 6. After first contact

- **Hold, seal, counterattack.** "A strong point is defended until the unit is formally ordered out of it." Divide it
  "into several independent, but mutually supporting, positions or sectors. If one … is overrun, limit the enemy
  penetration with obstacles and fires and support a counterattack." (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-27, 5-27a(3), p. 5-40.) "It is
  especially important for enemy footholds to be repelled violently" (¶5-12c(12)).
- **Fall back only as rehearsed.** "Rehearsals cover actions the platoon takes if it has to fall back to a second
  defensive perimeter" (FM 3-21.8 ¶8-129). Mouseholes are marked "once the defender has withdrawn to another level"
  (MCWP 3-35.3 App. D ¶2, p. D-9). "Clearing or defending a building is easier from an upper story"; an enemy "forced
  to the top … may be cornered and fight desperately" (App. A ¶12, p. A-17).
- **The ambush itself ends at initiation.** Fire is then ceased or shifted, and the assault element may sweep the kill
  zone (FM 3-21.8 ¶9-95). After initiation it is a fight, not an ambush.
- **Historical.** At Stalingrad, Soviet troops "remained as physically close to the Germans as possible … whenever
  they thought they spotted a German weakness, they immediately counterattacked" (Lewis, p. 37). In Fallujah: when the
  point man "opened a door to a back room" the squad took "intense, close-range automatic weapons fire from multiple
  insurgents". The insurgents then "broke contact, throwing a fragmentation grenade as they fled the building" (Peralta
  citation, **secondary**). Kasal's squad cleared a first room, then was hit "from an elevated enemy firing position"
  while moving to the next. Insurgents then threw grenades at the wounded (Kasal citation, **secondary**). Both houses
  were defended in depth: a back room, a firing position above, grenades, then withdrawal. The attackers' answer was
  firepower before entry, "instead of you taking it in the face on the way in the door, we can leave nothing but a
  charred room" (Matthews, p. 73, MAJ Karcher).

## 7. Grenades and stuns thrown in first

- **The attackers will throw if the room is held.** "Unless the ROE prevent it, use grenades before assaulting defended
  areas, moving through breaches, or entering unsecured areas" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14, p. 3-15). For a room "occupied by an
  alerted enemy force that is determined to resist … fragmentation grenades should be used to neutralize a space before
  friendly troops enter" (¶3-16e, p. 3-23). A stun is used "to momentarily distract the defender while they enter"
  (¶3-16b).
- **The numbers.** M84: "a flash of over one million candlepower, and a bang that is 170 to 180 decibels at 5 feet …
  disorientation, confusion, ear injuries, and temporary loss of hearing within 9 meters and up to 3 seconds of flash
  blindness" (TC 3-23.30 Table 2-7, p. 2-13). M67: "the killing radius is 5 meters and the casualty radius … 15 meters,
  fragments can disperse as far away as 230 meters" (¶B-4). A grenade through a house door covers the whole room.
- **How defenders protect against it.** A wall of sandbags, rubble or furniture "to the rear of the position to protect
  the soldier from explosions in the room", and a table or bedstead as overhead cover (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-29b(2)–(3),
  pp. 3-51–3-52). Windows are covered with mesh "to prevent grenades from being thrown in" (¶5-30d(2)). In the open,
  grenade sumps are dug (FM 3-21.8 ¶8-151). Against frags through thin walls, "lie flat on the floor with their helmet
  towards the area of detonation, or move away from any wall that might be penetrated" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14a(3), p. 3-17).
- **Throwing it back.** Attackers cook frags off "to prevent the enemy from throwing them back" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14).
  Marines skip-throw "so hard that it skips and bounces, making it difficult for the enemy to pick up and throw back"
  (MCWP 3-35.3 App. A ¶19c(1), p. A-29). Defenders throw their own grenades at the entry (Fallujah, §6).
- **Inference:** a post in the entered room, without hard cover between it and the door, is inside the frag's 5 m kill
  radius and the stun's 9 m effect. Doctrine's answers are hard cover beside the post, or covering the doorway from the
  next room or through a wall.

## What this means for the game

1. **All-round vs concentrate.** Sending *every* free member, leader included, to one doorway follows "unengaged
   elements reinforce" (FM 90-10-1 ¶4-26a). It breaks "all-around security first" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30b(1)) and the
   ambush's rear security (FM 3-21.8 ¶7-125). Closer to doctrine: keep one member (or members holding a defend order)
   on another approach when the crew has 3 or more, and send the rest. With 2, both going is a defensible game choice.
   Doctrine does not cover a crew that small.
2. **Posts.** Off the axis, back from the wall, out of the funnel, hidden from the entry's lookouts, crouched, sides
   alternated: each matches doctrine (¶3-22c(9), ¶3-28, ¶5-27a(2), FM 90-10-1 ¶4-14d(2)). The depths (0.4–1.6 m) are
   game estimates; doctrine gives none. Two cautions. A post tucked against the door's own wall is the first spot
   the buttonhook or hard-corner man reaches. And every in-room post sits inside a grenade's lethal radius.
3. **Acting on footsteps.** Ordering the ambush on a heard approach matches "look and listen … alert" (¶5-31b–c) and
   the ambush's report-then-alert chain (FM 3-21.8 ¶9-94). Ending the ambush at sight or fire, not at more footsteps,
   matches "initiate when most of the enemy is in the kill zone" (¶9-95).
4. **Leader in a post.** Doctrine supports it at fire-team scale: the team leader fights, the ambush leader is with the
   assault element. Real units plan the posts in advance. The game's on-the-spot order is the doctrinal "supplementary
   position occupied on specific enemy action" (FM 3-21.8 ¶8-20) without the rehearsal.
5. **Re-position cadence.** Re-order only on a real change of threatened doorway (a callout naming another door). Never
   re-order while the threat is at the door: doctrine calls displacement in contact the dangerous case (¶8-46). Hold a
   new post until the threat moves again.
6. **After contact.** "Fight on their own" matches decentralized execution (FM 3-06.11 p. 5-3, ¶5-12c(10)). Doctrine
   adds a violent counterattack on a foothold and a fall-back to the next room or floor. Fallujah's defenders fought
   from depth (back room, upstairs) and broke contact with grenades.
7. **Grenades.** Against a bang or frag the in-room ambush is the weak point. Doctrine puts hard cover between the
   defender and the door or holds the doorway from beyond it. In the game: rank low cover on the line to the
   threshold higher, and let the grenade-avoidance logic move ambushers when a bang lands (`docs/grenades.md`).
8. **Where the game must differ.** Its invaders use doorways that doctrine tells them to avoid (FM 90-10-1 ¶5-4; MCWP
   App. A §IV). Its defenders have no time to fortify: no sandbags, loopholes, mouseholes, wire or OP outside. Its
   rooms are smaller than any doctrinal distance, and its crew is a fire team where doctrine writes for a platoon.
