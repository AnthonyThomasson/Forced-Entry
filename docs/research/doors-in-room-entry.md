# Research: closed doors in room entry and room defence

Primary-source research for closed, hinged doors in houses (each opens one way, can be opened fully, cracked open by
degrees, and knocked down after enough damage). It feeds the door work against `docs/tactical-entry.md` (the stack,
the crossing, the corners), `docs/cover-and-stance.md` (what a door stops), `docs/grenades.md` (the bang through a
door), `docs/leadership.md` (the go at a held stack), `docs/hearing.md` (the noise of a door or a breach) and the
defenders' ambush (`docs/research/defending-a-room-ambush.md`). It covers how military fire teams and police entry
teams read a closed door, stack on it, open or breach it, fight through it and leave it behind, how defenders use
doors, and how wide a real door is. Scale for the game figures: 1 px ≈ 1 cm (as in `defending-a-room-ambush.md`).

**Source quality.** The three military manuals are doctrine and the strongest sources here; they give paragraph
numbers, and the 2011 ATTP is the most detailed on doors (it has a whole breaching section). Newer Army urban doctrine
was not checked, nor FM 3-34.214 (demolitions), which ATTP ¶7-74 refers to. The police items are course outlines
(JLE 140S, BART, SBCSD, SLO: topic lists, rarely the technique itself), a state standard (NV POST: organisation,
equipment and a glossary, not technique), the 1993 California POST basic-course guideline (real technique, but patrol
level and old) and trade press by serving or retired officers and trainers (Chapman, Nielsen, Papenfuhs, French,
Wolfe, Wood, Wills, Sumpter, Ijames, Griffith). Treat the police items as **common practice**, not doctrine. No agency
SOP that writes out door technique step by step was found; NTOA standards were not reachable as text. The two ALERRT
items are empirical studies (with students as suspects). The door sizes come from building codes (IRC, IBC, ADM), a
standard via a reference site (DIN 18101) and trade press (Bob Vila, AIBD) for the sizes doors are actually sold in.

## 1. Door types and where the stack stands

- **Which way it swings decides everything.** "A door is an obstacle to entering a room. To ensure the fastest and
  smoothest entry possible, the clearing team should line up on the side of the door that provides the least
  resistance to movement during entry. If a door opens inward, the clearing team lines up on the hinge side. If the
  door opens outward, the team lines up on the doorknob side. The number 1 man normally takes the path of least
  resistance to enter the room by pushing open the door, proceeding across the doorway, and engaging any enemy as he
  moves to his point of domination." (ATTP ¶7-99, p. 7-25.) The start point is "immediately adjacent to the entry point
  (doorway) on the side that supports the path of least resistance" (¶7-110, ¶7-112).
- **The swing also sets #1's direction inside.** "When using a doorway as the point of entry, the path of least
  resistance is determined initially based on the way the door opens; if the door opens inward he plans to move away
  from the hinges. If the door opens outward, he plans to move toward the hinged side." (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-22c(5)(a),
  p. 3-28.) So #1 crosses the doorway, into the half of the room the door leaf does not block.
- **Reading the swing is the first job at the door.** "Shooter Number One determines the direction in which the door
  opens then quickly informs Shooter Number Two and forces the door open." (MCWP app. A ¶19b, p. A-27.) Police name the
  same two cases: "Doors: push-and-go vs. pull-and-hold" (JLE 140S lab I.B.2; again for hallways, I.C.2 "Door
  openings: a. Pull and hold or, b. Push and go"); "Closed doors: a. Inward opening b. Outward opening" (BART II.5).
- **Push and pull are read from the team's side.** Common practice: "push" opens away from the team, "pull" toward
  it; "the striker side of a door is the side of the door with the knob or the opening mechanism", the hinge side the
  other (Chapman). Firefighters' rule of thumb, used in police breaching courses: hinges visible from your side means
  it opens toward you (fire-service material, not checked further).
- **What swings which way.** Common practice: "residential doors generally swing inward, whereas doors in commercial
  buildings typically swing outward"; "Exterior doors are typically solid core and swing inward, while interior doors
  are often hollow core" (Nielsen). Doctrine: "Emergency doors are reinforced metal doors that open outward"
  (ATTP ¶7-72 note); stairwell doors "are steel doors that open outward and close automatically" (¶7-177).
- **Split or stacked.** USMC pair on a closed door, split: "Shooter Number Two … positions himself opposite Shooter
  Number One on the other side of the door and away from the wall in a safe position that allows Shooter Number One to
  shoot the door-opening mechanism … No matter what method is used to open the door, Shooter Number Two should get in
  a position on the side of the door opposite Shooter Number One." (MCWP app. A ¶20a.) Stacked: "Shooter Number Two is
  behind Shooter Number One … the same if the door is already open or nonexistent" (¶19a). Police pair: both on the
  same side when uncleared hall lies beyond the door (one covers the long hall), one on each side otherwise (Chapman).
- **Doors are the stack's danger.** "Doors can be easily booby trapped or blocked. Entering through a door that is not
  booby trapped or within the line of fire of enemy personnel is normally the best way to enter a room. If a door is
  suspected of being booby trapped or if enemy personnel may be in the room, avoid or explosively breach the door."
  (ATTP ¶7-17.) "Soldiers should trace the doorframe with their hand or barrel of their weapon while their eyes look
  for booby traps" (¶7-18). Doorways "are normally covered by enemy fire" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-6; MCWP app. A ¶7).

## 2. Opening a closed door

- **Assume locked, try the handle.** "View any closed door as being locked and assume it must be breached to gain
  entry … However, based on mission variables, most close-in breaches begin with a manual attempt to open the door.
  The clearing team should understand that turning the doorknob can give away the element of surprise and draw fire."
  (ATTP ¶7-107, p. 7-26.) Even the shotgunner "should first turn the doorknob and attempt to open the door" (¶7-67).
  Police: "Try the door knob first. There is obviously no need to use a breaching tool if the door is not locked"
  (Nielsen); using tools on an unlocked door wastes time and warns the occupants (Chapman). USMC: where firing is
  restricted or no booby traps are expected, "using the door handle to open the door may be appropriate" (MCWP A ¶19b).
- **Dynamic: open and enter in one movement.** "When moving through a doorway, the number 1 man should push the door
  all the way to the wall to ensure no one is behind it. He should use his foot to push open the door and not his
  body, as equipment may catch on the door" (ATTP ¶7-97). "The actions required to open or to breach the entry point,
  employing a … hand grenade immediately prior to entry (if appropriate), and the actual entering of the room should
  be a seamlessly interconnected series of events" (¶7-109); "Ideally, the team arrives and passes through the entry
  point without having to stop" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-21). Police: "make sure the door is opened forcefully enough to swing
  through a full arc", but "A door-closing device may prevent a full swing" (Wood).
- **Opening by hand, out of the line of fire.** "When opening an unlocked door by hand, the assault team should be sure
  not to expose themselves to enemy fire through the door. The soldiers should stay close to one side of the doorway
  to minimize exposure in the open doorframe." (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14e(2), p. 3-20.)
- **Who opens a push door, who a pull door (police practice).** On a push door "the hinge-side operator is primary
  assaulter and the striker-side operator is breacher"; on a pull door "the hinge-side operator is breacher and the
  striker-side operator is primary assaulter". The pull-door breacher "won't pull the door into their face, but trap
  the door behind their body", which leaves him exposed, so the door is opened and trapped fast (Chapman). Hallway
  version: "If the right flanker is closest to the hinges, he will reach across the door and grab the knob … The
  appropriate flanker will then pull the door open and the other flanker will enter the room." (Papenfuhs.) Doctrine
  for a heavy outward self-closing door: "One Soldier should be dedicated to opening and then holding the door"
  (ATTP ¶7-177). A self-closer swinging back on the team is a known hazard (Papenfuhs).
- **What the primary does as it opens.** Both officers "orient their muzzle on the striker side seam of the door,
  prepared to fight as soon as the door cracks open" (Chapman). The opener's hands go back to his weapon once the
  door is open (Chapman); the military opener (#1) is himself the first man in (ATTP ¶7-99).
- **Behind the door.** "Man closest to hinge side of entry door clears behind door" (JLE 140S I.B.3.e, I.B.4.e).
  "Push doors open fully to wall to preclude suspect(s) hiding behind doors. If door will not open fully, look between
  hinge edge of door and the door jamb before entering." (CA POST LD23, A.1.q.) "Anyone standing there should be
  located by the failure of the door to swing all the way open"; look by placing the light's bezel "between the door
  and the door frame"; "whoever enters on the door side then double checks" (Wolfe).
- **Crack and peek: not found as such.** No source describes opening a closed door a few degrees to look in and then
  closing it or continuing. What exists:
  - Police listen and pause at the opened door: "After door is open, position yourself by side of entry and listen"
    (CA POST LD23, A.1.h); "hesitate for a brief moment after opening the door. An ensconced enemy might launch his
    ambush as soon as he sees the door swing open, so don't rush into the 'fatal funnel'" (Wood).
  - Looking without entering: the hinge-gap look above; mirrors ("Mirroring a corner or room before entering is one of
    the best ways to minimize exposure", Wills; "Pole mirror/mirror" in the entry kit, SBCSD II.D.6); "Under Door
    Cameras" and fibre-optic kit for tier-1 teams (NV POST 3.2.3, p. 20).
  - Clearing from the threshold once the door is open: "Limited penetration: A tactical maneuver where forward access
    by an entry team is limited to a measured and partial entry … Suspects … can be detained at the point of the entry
    and/or be called out" (NV POST, p. 38); "The limited penetration involves bringing your shooting arm into the room
    while only exposing your shooting eye(s)" (Wills); "Threshold Evaluation" (BART II.9.a); "approximately 85% of the
    room is visible from the door" (ALERRT 2021). "Port and cover: Breaching and/or removal of all obstructions/barriers
    within a window, door or wall … to … deploy FSDD and/or safely observe and cover a room's interior … from outside"
    (NV POST, p. 39). "Breach and hold" keeps the team at the opened entry point (NV POST, p. 36; French).
  - Stealth entry is the slow mode: "Slow and deliberate movement … Movement should be quiet … time consuming and often
    physically exhausting" (SBCSD VI.G.2).
  - Peeking versus pushing in: in a corner-fed room, simulated suspects hit a peeking officer 33 % of the time and a
    pushing one 44 % (not significant), but hits on the peeker were more often to the head (Peek/Push).
- **The door as a diversion.** "A close-in breach of one door or even the jiggling of a doorknob can provide a critical
  distraction while the clearing team enters through another door" (ATTP ¶7-162).

## 3. Breaching a locked door

- **Methods and when.** "Techniques range from the simple method of kicking in a door to the complex use of specialized
  demolitions" (ATTP ¶7-49). Four methods: mechanical, ballistic, explosive, manual (¶7-55–7-75); FM 3-06.11 lists
  "mechanical, ballistic, explosive, or thermal" (¶3-14e(1)). Civilians present: "consider using nonlethal means to
  execute the breach" (ATTP ¶7-51); precision conditions use "mechanical or other less destructive breaching methods"
  (¶7-141).
  - *Mechanical* (sledgehammer, bolt cutters, crowbar, ram, axe): "Always plan mechanical breaching as a backup to a
    ballistic or explosive breaching … The building material of the door is critical" (¶7-56). "With some doors, the
    methods of mechanical breaching are very slow. Therefore, units should plan on an enemy reacting to the breach
    activity. Breaching fragile doors using a sledgehammer is effective and rapid" and keeps surprise (¶7-57).
    Police: "use of a ram on inward opening doors, and use of a Halligan with and without a ram on outward opening
    doors" (SLO, course description); "law enforcement teams often make the initial breach with a ram" (Nielsen).
  - *Manual (kick).* "Manual breaching uses the foot or shoulder" (ATTP ¶7-75). "If none of these methods are
    available, soldiers can resort to kicking the door open. This method is the least preferred since it is difficult
    and tiring to the soldier. It rarely works the first time, and gives any enemy soldiers in the room ample warning
    and time to shoot through the door." (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14e(1); same in MCWP A ¶19b.) Police: hollow-core doors "can be
    kicked open", "Avoid trying to kick in solid core exterior doors", "Steel doors or frames are not worth the effort",
    and "A dull thud when your foot hits the door likely indicates a reinforced structure" (Nielsen).
  - *Ballistic (shotgun).* "A shotgun can breach most interior wooden doors quickly. Metal doors and exterior wooden
    doors can also be breached with a shotgun but typically require additional shots." (ATTP ¶7-61.) Rifles "have
    limited breaching value" (¶7-60). Two aim points: "The doorknob breach is the preferred technique as it takes the
    least amount of shots"; a deadbolt, chain or bar may still hold, so "A hinge breach is typically executed only after
    discovering a door has multiple additional locking mechanisms" (¶7-66). "Never target the doorknob itself" (¶7-67).
    Wooden hinges take one to two shots each, metal two to three (¶7-70). The gunner "is announcing his presence by
    using the shotgun and is completely exposed to fire through the door" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-20a); "buckshot and rifled
    slugs can overpenetrate doors and may kill or wound occupants" (¶3-20a NOTE). Police: "Frangible breaching rounds
    should always be employed", used "when speed is essential or when other methods have failed" (Nielsen).
  - *Explosive.* "Often the fastest and most combat-effective method … After placing the demolition charge, all
    personnel should move a safe distance away to covered positions. Immediately after detonation, the clearing team
    moves to the entry point and enters" (ATTP ¶7-74). Door charges: "doorknob charge", "flexible linear charge",
    "rubber strip charge" and others (FM 3-06.11 ¶8-11). A rifle-launched entry munition (GREM) lets the team stay
    "behind cover until the GREM breaches the door" from 15–40 m (ATTP ¶7-71). Police: "most agencies will likely not
    have the need for it" (Nielsen); "the most problematic breaching method both for SWAT and for police
    administrators who worry about liability" (Griffith); NV POST asks only tier-1 teams to include it (§3.1.1).
- **Door classes doctrine uses.** FM 3-06.11 Table 8-5 sizes charges by door: "Hollow-core door", "Particle-filled door
  (1 inch)", "Solid wood door (2 inches)", "High-quality solid door", "Light metal door", "Medium steel door", "Heavy
  steel door". "Most residential doors are 80 inches tall and commercial doors are 84 inches tall" (¶8-11c(1)).
- **What the breached door does.**
  - Lock shot out: "the gunner immediately angles the shotgun upward, pushes or kicks in the door, and moves away from
    the door" (ATTP ¶7-68); "Doors may not always open after firing. The gunner should be prepared to kick the door"
    (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-20a(1)); after either technique "he kicks the door in or pulls it out" (¶3-20a(2)).
  - Hinges shot out: "In many cases defeating the two upper hinges causes the door to collapse from its own weight,
    pulling the lower hinge out of the door jam" (ATTP ¶7-70).
  - Launched charge: "against metal doors, the GREM causes the door to buckle inward and dislocate from the doorframe.
    When used against wooden doors, the GREM breaks the door into several pieces" (ATTP ¶7-72). On an outward-opening
    emergency door "the hinges and bolts could bend and get stuck, jamming the door into its frame" (¶7-72 note).
  - Placed charges: the flexible linear charge "cuts through the door near the hinges" (FM 3-06.11 ¶8-11c); the rubber
    strip charge "defeats the locking mechanism and dislodges the door from the frame", or on a metal door "will
    buckle and or bend the door" (¶8-11e); "All doorknobs and prop-sticks will become secondary missiles" (Table 8-7
    note). Police device: "blows doors in" (Griffith).
  - Large-calibre fire "readily opens any door" and kills occupants, with extensive collateral damage (ATTP ¶7-73).
- **The breacher in the file.** Shotgun: "the gunner up front, followed by the number 1 man, number 2 man (team
  leader), and then the number 3 man. After the door is breached, the gunner moves to the rear of the lineup and
  assumes the position of the number 4 man." Explosive: "The number 2 man (team leader) carries the demolition charge
  and places it"; #1 secures the doorway, #3 overhead, #4 rear; then all move to cover (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-20d(1)–(2)).
  ATTP: "Ideally, a separate breach element executes the breach, if not then Soldier 3 and Soldier 4 is the breach
  team" (¶7-112). Police: the team assigns roles before the approach, "One person … to knock/notice", "One person … to
  the pick", "One person … to the ram" (SBCSD V.A.3); a tool-carrying breacher steps aside and rejoins the stack
  (Chapman).
- **The doorway after the breach.** The gunner's move away and raised muzzle "signals that the doorway is cleared and
  allows the first Soldier entry and a clear shot into the room" (ATTP ¶7-68, ¶7-70). "Immediately after the breach,
  the breach team clears the doorway and, if previously deemed necessary, a hand grenade is thrown" (¶7-112).
  "Executing a close-in breach is the signal to begin entering" (¶7-84); "Quick entry is required to take advantage of
  the effects of the blast and concussion and to prevent the enemy from reorienting on the breach" (¶7-76). Start
  positions: adjacent to the door if there is no danger from the breach, otherwise a covered position away from it
  (¶7-79). Police plan for a "failed breach" (NV POST §2.1, §3.1.1) and pick a secondary entry because "The door/window
  could be fortified" (SBCSD V.A.2.b).

## 4. Doors under fire

- **Interior doors are concealment, not cover.** "A residential interior door may provide concealment, but it provides
  no bullet cover." (Sumpter, common practice.) Doctrine says it of the walls around them: "an interior wall made of
  thin wood paneling, sheetrock, or plaster is no protection against 5.56-mm rounds" (ATTP ¶B-28). "That sheetrock wall
  doesn't stop bullets any better than the door does" (Chapman). Even a "3/8-inch mild steel door" is penetrated by a
  single 5.56 round at 25–100 m (FM 3-06.11 Table 7-2) and by 7.62 ball (Table 7-5).
- **Doors do stop fragments.** "Fragmentation barriers consisting of common office furniture, mattresses, doors, or
  books can be effective against the fragmentation grenade inside rooms" (FM 3-06.11 ¶7-8b(3)).
- **Defenders shoot through closed doors.** Kicking gives the room "time to shoot through the door" (FM 3-06.11
  ¶3-14e(1)); the shotgunner "is completely exposed to fire through the door" (¶3-20a); the opener must "not expose
  themselves to fires through the door" (MCWP A ¶19b). News: a suspect fired through a door as deputies broke its glass,
  wounding two (Hernando).
- **Attackers shoot through doors to open them, not to clear rooms.** USMC: "The two-man clearing team forces the door
  open by directing a short burst of automatic fire through the door around the latch … and then kicking it open"
  (MCWP A ¶19b, fig. A-30). The Army calls rifles "not recommended for breaching" (ATTP ¶7-60). No source tells an
  entry team to fire blind through a closed door at unseen occupants; the shotgun note on overpenetration warns
  against hitting occupants (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-20a).
- **The fatal funnel is the open doorway.** "The door is the focal point of anyone in the room. It is known as the
  fatal funnel" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-21a). "During entry, the clearing team should minimize their exposure to potential
  enemy fire through the fatal funnel by rapidly moving through the opening and staying close to one side of the
  opening" (ATTP ¶7-52). "Pausing in a doorway gives the bad guys the perfect target" (Wills). Immediate threats
  include enemy "within 3 to 5 feet of the breach point" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-21c). Points of domination are "at least one
  meter to the left and to the right of the entry point" and never "in front of doors" (ATTP ¶7-94).

## 5. Grenades and stuns with doors

- **Open or breach the door, then throw.** "Once the door is breached, a grenade should precede the soldier's entry"
  (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14e(1)); "Once the door is open, a hand grenade should be tossed in. After the grenade explodes,
  soldiers enter" (¶3-14e(3)). USMC: "Once the door is open, Shooter Number Two immediately throws a hand grenade into
  the room. Shooter Number One maintains cover … Shooter Number Two then returns to the original stacked position"
  (MCWP A ¶19c). ATTP: after the breach "the breach team clears the doorway and … a hand grenade is thrown"
  (¶7-112); "if the enemy is suspected to be in the room or has actually fired from the room, throw a flash-bang hand
  grenade into the room after the breach and before entry … In the event a fragmentation hand grenade is thrown, you
  may need to wait for several minutes for the dust and debris to settle before entry" (¶7-52). The M84 stun "is
  designed to be thrown into a room (through an open door, a standard glass window, or other opening)" (FM 3-06.11
  ¶7-8b(5)). A stairwell door is engaged "with a grenade … let it detonate, and quickly move inside" (¶3-14c).
- **Police look first.** "Peek into room before throwing" (JLE 140S I.B.6.c); "The user MUST confirm positively that
  all areas are safe prior to tossing the device", and not "pitch the device blindly immediately following the breach"
  (Ijames). NV POST's "port and cover" opens a door or window to "deploy FSDD" from outside (p. 39); "Bang Poles"
  deliver the device without opening the door wide (equipment list §3.2.2, p. 19).
- **Closing the door after the throw: not found.** No manual or police item has the thrower shut the door again
  before the bang; doctrine enters on the detonation. Frags hurt the team outside: "fragments from M67 fragmentation
  grenades may injure Marines outside the room" (MCWP A ¶19c warning). A closed door would stop fragments (FM 3-06.11
  ¶7-8b(3)) but no source uses it that way.

## 6. Defenders and doors

- **Shut, block and reinforce what is not used.** "Unused doors should be locked or nailed shut, as well as blocked
  and reinforced with furniture, sandbags, or other field expedients" (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30d(1)(a)). USMC adds "Outside
  doors can be boobytrapped" (MCWP D ¶2c(1)(a)). "Block stairwells and doors with wire or other material to prevent
  or delay enemy" (MCWP ¶3104; FM 3-06.11 ¶5-13d(8) "Block stairwells and doorways with wire").
- **Fight through a barricaded door.** "The natural firing port provided by windows and doors can be improved by
  barricading the window or door and leaving a small hole for the Soldier's use … Barricade all ground-floor doors
  and windows, unless they are critical for friendly movement." (ATTP ¶7-200.) "It is better to leave two or three
  firing ports in each door and window … Firing from the bottom of the window or door … makes the firing port less
  obvious" (¶7-201). "Upon occupying a building, soldiers board up all windows and doors. By leaving small gaps between
  the slots, soldiers can use windows and doors as good alternate positions" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-29m). "Wire mesh covering
  the window or door hampers the enemy from throwing in hand grenades" (ATTP ¶7-214 list).
- **Doors are where the enemy looks.** Defenders keep doors "under observation and fire" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-29l) and
  doctrine expects "booby traps on doorways and windows" (FM 3-06.11 ¶1-6). Positions should "Avoid the
  area immediately around a window, doorway, or loophole" and fire "from the shadowed interior of a room" (ATTP
  ¶7-214 list).
- **The ambush is at the moment the door opens.** "An ensconced enemy might launch his ambush as soon as he sees the
  door swing open" (Wood). "If your adversary is waiting to ambush you in a well-fortified stronghold it may not matter
  what tactic you use. They have an advantage because they own the ground" (French). Behind the door itself: "It's an
  easy place for a bad guy to hide" (Wood); hence the hinge-side check (Wolfe; JLE 140S I.B.3.e). The doorway tells the
  suspect where officers must come in (Peek/Push summary).
- **Where occupants wait.** In ALERRT's study of hiding suspects, "Forty-five percent of the time the participants
  positioned themselves along the wall that is visible from the door", "34%" in a blind corner, "21%" in the centre;
  "Sixteen percent of the time the participants were fully concealed and could not be seen from the threshold"
  (ALERRT 2021).
- **A held room with its door shut** is the police "barricaded subject": "the standoff created by an armed or
  potentially armed suspect in any location, whether fortified or not, who is refusing to comply" (NV POST, p. 36). No
  doctrine was found on defenders deliberately keeping an interior door shut and waiting for it to open; the closest is
  the military rule to block unused doors and fight from ports in barricaded ones.

## 7. Moving through a house with doors

- **Closed doors along a hall are called and covered.** In the serpentine, #2 and #3 cover "any enemies who appear
  suddenly from nearby doorways on either side of the hall" (ATTP ¶7-31). Police hallway team: "The point man calls
  out, 'Closed door, right'"; the flankers squeeze forward and "Both the point man and right flanker move past the
  door", then the flankers, "in a crossfire should someone suddenly exit the room", decide who opens it while "The
  point man and the rear guard remain in the hallway" (Papenfuhs). BART teaches "Opposing Doors" as its own hallway
  case (II.7.d); SBCSD lists "Open doors" and "Closed doors" as separate threat areas (VI.E). A covered landing door is
  covered "from the doorknob side" (ATTP ¶7-182).
- **A team holds only where it stands.** "They can only secure where they are, not where they have been" (ATTP ¶7-28).
  Rooms are cleared and then marked "in accordance with unit SOP", with the team checking the room's other doors
  (¶7-128).
- **Doors left behind: military open, police patrol closed.** USMC: "As rooms are cleared, doors should be left open,
  and a predetermined mark (cloth, tape, spray paint) should be placed on the doorjamb or over the door" (MCWP C ¶6h).
  Army marking SOP: "Markings should be placed on the upper left side of the door" (FM 3-06.11 app. I, fig. I-2).
  California patrol: "Close doors after each room has been thoroughly searched"; "Make sure each room is searched
  thoroughly before searching another. Close doors of rooms already searched." (CA POST LD23, K and A.1.s.) The
  military rule marks progress for following units; the patrol rule keeps a suspect from slipping back into a
  searched room (inference; neither source states its reason).
- **Hasty searches behind closed doors.** No source sets a hasty procedure that skips opening a closed door. Police
  "Check behind all doors and search any area large enough to conceal a suspect" (CA POST LD23, J). Two officers per
  room minimum (SBCSD VI.D.2). The four-man drill wants "a door or a door-size wall breach" (ATTP ¶7-106); a wall
  breach should be "a door-size opening" entered "using the same procedures as when entering through a doorway"
  (¶7-26).

- **Clear width is the leaf less the stop and the leaf's own thickness:** about 5 cm in the UK figure (826 → 775 mm,
  ADM) and up to 10 cm in the US figure (36 → 32 in, AIBD). So a typical US 32-in interior door gives about 71–76 cm
  clear and a 36-in front door about 81–86 cm (inference from those two figures).
- **Shoulder width.** ANSUR II bideltoid breadth (the widest point across the upper arms): men mean **51.0 cm**
  (5th–95th percentile 45.9–56.7 cm), women mean **45.0 cm** (40.6–49.9 cm); chest depth men 25.4 cm (computed from the
  public ANSUR II files). Body armour and kit add to this (not measured here).
- **One at a time.** A 71–86 cm opening is about 1.4–1.7 male shoulder widths; two men abreast need about 102 cm
  before kit. A real door passes one person at a time, which is why doctrine speaks of the "fatal funnel" and moves the
  team through it in file (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-21a; ATTP ¶7-96 "Clearing team stacks should be very tight").

## Implications for the game

1. **Swing decides the stack's side.** Stack on the hinge side of a push door and the knob side of a pull door
   (ATTP ¶7-99); #1 then crosses to the half of the room the leaf does not block (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-22c(5)(a)). This is
   the one rule every source agrees on, and it gives each one-way door a "good side" for the entry geometry.
2. **The opener is a role, read off the swing.** Push door: #1 kicks it open to the wall and goes in, no stop
   (ATTP ¶7-97). Pull door: someone must pull and hold it (the hinge-side member traps it behind his body) while the
   other side enters (Chapman; Papenfuhs; JLE 140S "pull-and-hold"). A held door costs that member his first shot.
3. **Try the handle, then breach.** A closed door is treated as locked; the handle is tried first and that is itself
   a noise that can draw fire (ATTP ¶7-107). A locked door needs a breach: a kick is the slow, noisy, often-repeated
   last resort that invites fire through the door; a tool or shotgun breach is a few seconds by one breacher, and the
   breach itself is the go (ATTP ¶7-84; FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14e(1)). "Damage until it gives" maps to kicks and rams; hinges
   shot through give a door that falls (ATTP ¶7-70).
4. **Crack-and-peek is police common practice, not doctrine.** Doctrine opens and enters in one move; the cautious
   police mode opens, pauses, listens, looks through the hinge gap or with a mirror, and clears from the threshold
   (CA POST; Wood; Wolfe; NV POST limited penetration). A gradual-opening door fits the quiet / deliberate mode;
   the dynamic mode opens it fully at once. The defender's moment is the swing (Wood).
5. **Doors hide, they do not protect.** Interior doors are concealment only (Sumpter; ATTP ¶B-28); bullets should
   pass through a closed interior door, sight should not. Defenders firing through a door at a team working on it is
   realistic (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14e(1)); an entry team firing blind through it is not in any source, beyond the USMC burst
   at the latch to open it.
6. **Bang through an opened door, not a closed one.** The door is opened or breached first, the stun goes in, the team
   goes on the bang (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-14e(3); ATTP ¶7-112). Closing the door again after the throw is not in any source.
7. **Defenders use doors as obstacles and ports.** Unused doors locked, blocked, booby-trapped; barricaded doors fought
   through small ports; the ambush waits for the door to swing (FM 3-06.11 ¶5-30d(1)(a); ATTP ¶7-200; Wood).
8. **Doors behind the team: pick a rule.** Military leaves cleared doors open and marked (MCWP C ¶6h); police patrol
   closes searched rooms (CA POST). Either is defensible; open-and-marked is the doctrine for a team that will not be
   coming back.
9. **Size.** The game's scale is set by the body, not by 1 px ≈ 1 cm: a character is 28 px across (radius 14), the
   width of a 51 cm bideltoid breadth, so 1 m is about 55 px (the skeleton agrees: a 7.2-radius head height, 100 px,
   for a 1.75 m man). A real interior doorway is then about 39–42 px clear (leaf about 45 px) and a front door about
   45–47 px clear (leaf 50 px). The doorways were 80–120 px (1.5–2.2 m); #21 shrinks them to 52 px inside and 60 px at
   the front and exits (about 0.95 m and 1.1 m), a little roomier than real doors, which still passes one body at a
   time (the nav lane through a 52 px doorway is 52 − 2 × (14 + 4) = 16 px wide).
