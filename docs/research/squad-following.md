# Research: how a small team follows its leader

Primary-source research for the coordinated-squads work (members follow their leader). It covers a fire team
or police entry team of 3–5 people. Scale used for the game column: 1 px ≈ 1 cm.

## 1. Formation and spacing behind the leader

- **Wedge is the basic Army fire-team formation, 10 m interval.** "The interval between Soldiers in the wedge
  formation is normally 10 meters. The wedge expands and contracts depending on the terrain." (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-34,
  p. 3-8.)
- **Terrain and visibility shrink it to a file.** "The normal interval is reduced so all team members can still see
  their team leader … The sides of the wedge can contract to the point where the wedge resembles a single file.
  Soldiers expand or resume their original positions when moving in less rugged terrain." (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-34.)
- **The file is the indoor formation.** "Team leaders use the file when employing the wedge is impractical. This
  formation is most often used in severely restrictive terrain, like inside a building … The distance between
  Soldiers in the column changes due to constraints of the situation, particularly when in urban operations."
  (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-36, p. 3-9.) No metre figure is given for indoors.
- **Dispersion is set by control, not a number.** "The distance between men will be determined by the mission, the
  nature of the threat, the closeness of the terrain, and by the visibility. As a general rule, the unit should be
  dispersed up to the limit of control." (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-33, p. 3-8.)
- **Limited visibility.** Leaders "reduce the interval between Soldiers and between units to make sure they can see
  each other" and "conduct headcounts at regular intervals and after each halt." (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-173, p. 3-48.)
- **Urban, between buildings: 3–5 m.** "Team members should keep a distance of 3 to 5 meters between themselves
  and, using a planned signal, make an abrupt flanking movement (on line) across the open area." (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-8,
  p. 3-7.)
- **Inside a building.** "When moving in hallways, he never moves alone—he always moves with at least one other
  soldier for security. The soldier should try to stay 12 to 18 inches away from walls." (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-25a,
  p. 3-39.) Hallway formations are the **serpentine** (diamond: #1 front, #2/#3 the sides, #4 rear) and the
  **rolling-T** (vee) (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-25b; FM 3-21.8 ¶7-199–7-200, p. 7-42). The police course calls the diamond
  "a movement formation" that "masses personnel in the hallway, a fatal funnel". Its fighting alternative
  ("Rolling Thunder") is two columns along the walls (JLE 140S lab I.C.1).
- **USMC puts no number on it.** "It is not important that exact distances and intervals be maintained between fire
  teams and individuals as long as control is not lost. Sight or voice contact will be maintained within the fire
  team." (MCWP 3-11.2 ¶3102a, p. 3-1.) In the USMC **column**, the rifleman leads and the fire team leader is
  **second**, followed by the automatic rifleman and then the assistant automatic rifleman. In the USMC **wedge**,
  the rifleman is at the point and "the position of the fire team leader and assistant automatic rifleman are
  interchangeable" (MCWP 3-11.2 fig. 3-1, 3-2, pp. 3-2–3-3; symbol legend fig. 1-1). Each fire team leader picks
  his own formation (¶3102a).

## 2. The short halt

- **Cigar-shaped perimeter, without an order.** "When the unit halts, if terrain permits, Soldiers should move off
  the route and face out to cover the same sectors of fire they were assigned while moving, allowing passage
  through the center of the formation … Actions by subordinate leaders and their Soldiers occur without an order
  from the leader." (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-162, p. 3-46.)
- **Each soldier's actions at a halt** (FM 3-21.8 Table 3-7, p. 3-47, "These actions occur without leader
  prompting"): move to the best available cover; visually inspect and clear the immediate surroundings,
  **"roughly 5–25m radius"**; establish a sector of fire with "12 o'clock as the direction the Soldier is facing",
  **from 10 o'clock to 2 o'clock**; coordinate with the soldiers on the left and right. The squad leader clears
  "about **35m**, the distance within hand grenade range", and makes the sectors overlap "with no gaps". When
  operating independently, he establishes **360-degree, three-dimensional security**.
- **A fire team on its own** "when halted, they use a perimeter defense to ensure all-around security"
  (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-33).
- **Distance from the leader.** Doctrine does not pull members in around the leader at a halt. They keep their
  movement interval and positions, adjusted for cover. The only constraint is control: members can see the
  leader (¶3-34/3-35), or keep sight or voice contact (MCWP ¶3102a). Leaders count heads after each halt (¶3-173).
- **"Follow me and do as I do."** "This simple formation permits the fire team leader to lead by example. The
  leader's standing order to his Soldiers is: 'Follow me and do as I do.' When he moves to the right, his Soldiers
  should also move to the right. When he fires, his Soldiers also fire. When using the lead-by-example technique,
  it is essential for all Soldiers to maintain visual contact with the leader." (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-35, p. 3-8; also
  ¶1-73, p. 1-14: the team leader "provides C2 through leadership by example ('Do as I do')".)

## 3. How far members may separate in a fight

- **No metric dispersion limit at fire-team level.** The binding rules are visual contact with the leader
  (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-35) and sight or voice contact (MCWP 3-11.2 ¶3102a), with dispersion "up to the limit of control"
  (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-33).
- **Bounding.** Individual movement is "high and low crawl, and three to five second rushes from one covered
  position to another" (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-65, p. 3-19). Bounds are set by METT-TC, but the bounding element "should
  never move beyond the range at which the base-of-fire squad(s) can effectively suppress known, likely, or
  suspected enemy positions. In severely restrictive terrain, the bounding squad(s) makes shorter bounds"
  (¶3-84, p. 3-26). "Ideally, the overwatch element maintains visual contact with the bounding element" (¶3-88,
  p. 3-27). The bounding team "signals the squad leader when his team completes its bound" (¶3-73, p. 3-22).
- **Squad-level spacing for scale.** In traveling overwatch, "Ideally, the lead team moves at least 50 meters in
  front of the rest of the element" (FM 3-21.8 ¶3-72, p. 3-21).
- **Indoors, the limit is pairs and sectors.** Nobody moves alone in a hallway (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-25a). In a room,
  members stop "1 meter from the #1-man's muzzle" when collapsing sectors (JLE 140S lab I.B.3). "No movement
  should mask the fire of any of the other team members" (FM 3-21.8 ¶7-195).

## 4. Bringing the team to a doorway

- **The stack forms before the door, in entry order.** "Clearing team members must approach the breach point
  quickly, quietly, and in standard order … The members of the fire team are assigned numbers 1 through 4, with
  the **team leader normally designated number 2**. If one member … is armed with the SAW … he should be
  designated number 4." (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-20d, p. 3-26.) The order changes with the breach: for a shotgun breach the
  gunner goes first and then moves to the rear as #4.
- **Ideally no stop at the door.** "If possible, the team moves from a covered or concealed position already in
  their entry order. Ideally, the team arrives and passes through the entry point without having to stop."
  (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-21, p. 3-27.) "Unless a grenade is being thrown prior to entry, the team should avoid stopping
  outside the point of entry." (¶3-22c(4).)
- **Who signals.** "All team members must signal one another that they are ready before the team moves to the
  entry point", with no voice if surprise matters (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-22c(3)(b)–(c), p. 3-29). In the police course,
  "the #2-man controls the movement into the next room" and "#2-man initiates entry by squeezing #1-man"
  (JLE 140S lab I.A.5, I.B.1). Both sources put the go with the #2, who is normally the team leader.
- **The grenade or flashbang decision.** In the Army foothold drill, the **squad leader** commands "COOK OFF
  GRENADES", or "if the squad leader decides not to use grenades, he commands, PREPARE TO ENTER—GO!" (FM 3-21.8
  ¶7-190, pp. 7-39–7-40). In room clearing, "The number 2 man may throw a grenade of some type (fragmentation,
  concussion, stun) into the room before entry", consistent with ROE and building structure (FM 3-06.11
  ¶3-22c(4)(a)–(b), p. 3-29). The police course signals a distraction device by hand and has the thrower "show it
  to number 1-man prior to throwing … Peek into room before throwing" (JLE 140S lab I.B.6). So the bang is a team
  or leader call that depends on the situation (ROE, structure, noncombatants). Leaders also adapt the drill itself:
  for consistently small rooms, "clear with two or three men" (FM 3-06.11 Table 3-1, p. 3-33).
- **Inside, each man keeps his own drill.** Directions are not preplanned; each man goes opposite the man in front
  of him. #1 takes the path of least resistance (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-22c(5)(a)–(b); FM 3-21.8 ¶7-196). "Once the room
  is cleared, the team leader signals to the squad leader" (FM 3-06.11 ¶3-22c(11)). On scene, the team leader
  holds "compromise authority" to act on a suspect's actions (NV POST glossary, p. 37).

## 5. Succession when the leader falls

- **Army: the automatic rifleman.** The AR "is normally the senior Soldier of the fire team" and must "be prepared
  to assume the duties of the team leader and squad leader" (FM 3-21.8 ¶1-76, p. 1-16). The grenadier is prepared
  to take over as AR and as team leader (¶1-75), and the rifleman likewise (¶1-74, p. 1-15).
- **USMC: everyone can step up one.** "Every man of a fire team must know the duties of the other team members, and
  in turn, the fire team leader and the squad leader should be able to assume the duties of their next superior."
  (MCWP 3-11.2 ¶1005a, p. 1-4.) The AAR "is prepared to assume the duties of the automatic rifleman" (¶1005e,
  p. 1-5). The manual names no fixed successor to the fire team leader.
- **Re-forming.** The casualty is called with "MAN DOWN!" (FM 3-06.11 Table 3-2, p. 3-46). In reorganisation the
  unit is "ensuring key leadership positions are filled if the operators/crew become casualties", and
  "reestablishing the chain of command" heads the reorganisation list (FM 3-21.8 ¶7-27 and Table 7-1, p. 7-10).
  Leaders take headcounts after each halt (¶3-173). The team re-forms on the new leader by the same "follow me"
  rule. No doctrine describes a geometric re-gather; the new leader resumes the formation.

## Where the game's rules differ

1. **File spacing is stack-tight everywhere.** At 55 + 45·i px (0.55 m, then 0.45 m between centres, with
   0.17–0.27 m of air between 0.28 m bodies), the column is a **stack** at all times. That is right at a door
   (the police "squeeze" means touching) but 6–20× tighter than doctrine elsewhere: 3–5 m between buildings and
   10 m in the open wedge. Indoors, doctrine gives no figure. A defensible game rule would scale with space,
   e.g. a few metres outdoors and about 1–1.5 m in halls and rooms. That last range is a design estimate, not
   doctrine.
2. **The leader leads the file in the game.** That matches the Army ("follow me"). It does not match the USMC column
   (scout first, leader second) or any entry stack, where the **team leader is normally #2** and gives the go by
   squeezing #1.
3. **The halt ring at ~0.8 m behind the leader is not doctrine.** Real members hold their movement positions, step to
   cover and **face out across the sectors they held while moving** (10–2 o'clock each, 360° overall), without an
   order. They do not converge on the leader. "Facing outward" matches; the gather-in and the 0.8 m radius do not.
   Indoors, a tight ring is closer to the cigar perimeter's intent, provided no one blocks the corridor ("allowing
   passage through the center").
4. **A metric leash (3.2 m, re-decide past 4.16 m) has no doctrinal analogue.** Doctrine leashes by **line of sight
   or voice to the leader**, plus overwatch range for bounds. A radius of 3.2 m is plausible inside a house, where
   sight is lost within a room or two. Outdoors it is far tighter than the 10 m normal interval. A sight-or-voice
   check, with the radius as a fallback, would be closer to the real rule.
5. **Succession.** If the game promotes by distance or index, doctrine says the AR (senior soldier) takes over in
   the Army; the USMC says the next man steps up. Either way, "MAN DOWN!", then re-establish the chain, then resume
   following.
6. **Entry autonomy matches.** Doctrine leaves the bang or grenade decision to the situation and the leader (ROE,
   structure, noncombatants) and the room drill to each man's position. That is consistent with followers keeping
   their own entry technique.
